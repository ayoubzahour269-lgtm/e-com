## 5 AI PROMPTS Every PhD Student Needs

5 AI PROMPTS Every PhD Student Needs
(copy-paste templates )
If you’re still typing “summarize this paper” into ChatGPT, you’re leaving 80% of the value on the table.

- -

—
01. THE SOURCE CONNECTOR
For when you have 15 tabs open and no idea how the papers relate.
Copy paste prompt:
“I’m writing a literature review on [YOUR TOPIC]. I’ll give you summaries of several papers one at a time. After each one, don’t just summarize, tell me how it agrees, disagrees, or adds a new angle compared to the papers I’ve already given you. At the end, help me group them into 2 to 3 themes I can use as subheadings.”
Why it works: You are forcing the model to compare, not repeat. Comparison is the actual academic skill a literature review is supposed to show, so this prompt trains the output toward synthesis instead of summary.
—
02. THE BLIND SPOT HUNTER
This is the one that saves you months. Every supervisor asks “what’s the gap” and every student freezes.
Copy paste prompt:
“Based on these papers on [YOUR TOPIC], act as a critical reviewer. List what has been studied extensively, what has been studied but with conflicting results, and what hasn’t been studied at all. Then suggest 3 possible research questions that address an actual gap, not just a variation of existing work.”
Why it works: Gap finding is a comparison task across many sources at once, something the human brain struggles to hold in memory but AI handles easily. Naming the gap this clearly also gives you a ready answer the moment your supervisor asks.
—
03. THE COMMITTEE SIMULATOR
Before you defend your methodology to your committee, defend it to AI first.
Copy paste prompt:
“Here is my proposed research methodology: [PASTE METHODOLOGY]. Play the role of a strict thesis committee member. Identify any weaknesses in sample size, validity, bias, or method choice. Ask me the 3 toughest questions a reviewer might ask about this methodology.”
Why it works: Role play prompts push AI to adopt a critical stance instead of a supportive one, which surfaces weaknesses you would otherwise only discover during the real defense.
—
04. THE FORMAL VOICE FIXER
For when you know what you want to say, but it comes out sounding like a text message instead of a thesis.
Copy paste prompt:
“Rewrite the following paragraph in formal academic tone suitable for a peer reviewed journal in [YOUR FIELD]. Keep all the original meaning and citations, but improve sentence structure, remove casual language, and ensure it reads as objective and precise: [PASTE YOUR PARAGRAPH]”
Why it works: This isolates tone and structure from content, so the AI is polishing your ideas rather than inventing new ones. That keeps your original argument intact while fixing the delivery.
—
05. THE EXAMINER ROLEPLAY
The one you wish existed when you were defending my own PhD.
Copy paste prompt:
“I’m preparing for my PhD viva or defense. My thesis is about [ONE LINE SUMMARY]. Act as an external examiner. Ask me 5 questions ranging from easy to very difficult, covering my methodology, my contribution to the field, and potential weaknesses. After each answer I give you, tell me honestly if a real examiner would accept it or push further.”
Why it works: Live back and forth simulation trains your recall under pressure, which is closer to how a real viva feels than reading prepared answers off a page.
📌 PRO TIPS
• Feed AI your own draft first, never let it write content it hasn’t seen from you
• Push back when an answer feels generic, ask it to get specific to your field
• Do the viva prompt out loud, not just typed, treat it like a real rehearsal
• Run the methodology prompt before you submit your proposal, not after
• Always fact check anything AI generates, never let it invent citations
• Save every good AI conversation as a reusable template for your next chapter
🔍 THE TRUTH
AI will not write your thesis for you, and it should not. What it actually does well is speed up the thinking work around your thesis: spotting patterns across papers, stress testing your logic, and cleaning up your language so your ideas land clearly. The students getting real value from these tools are not asking AI to think for them, they are using it as a sparring partner that pushes back. Treat it like a smart but overconfident research assistant who needs fact checking, and it becomes genuinely useful.
🚀 QUICK START
• Pick one prompt above that matches what you are stuck on right now
• Copy it exactly, then fill in your topic or paste your own text
• Run it in ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, results vary slightly between tools
• Read the output critically, do not accept the first answer as final
• Ask a follow up question to push the AI deeper before you move on
• Save the prompt somewhere you will actually find it again next week
🎯 BOTTOM LINE
These prompts will not replace your thinking, but they will remove the friction that keeps you stuck for hours. Use them as a starting point, not a finish line, and your thesis moves faster with less stress.
DON’T JUST SCROLL.
USE THESE.
Save this post, screenshot the 5 prompts, and actually use them this week - not just watch and forget.

## 7 Claude Prompt that Build You A Premium Website

Most people still use lazy prompts. That’s why they end up with generic layouts, the same purple gradient everyone else has, and default fonts that scream “I used a builder.”
Use these 7 prompts instead. Save this post. You’ll want it later. 🔖

1. The Signature Blueprint Prompt
“Act as a senior website strategist and UX architect. I want a website for [business type] that feels intentionally designed and custom built, not assembled from a template. First, ask me up to 5 sharp clarifying questions about my brand personality, audience, offer, and visual style. Then give me: the exact page structure a polished site should use, which sections most template sites skip, the layout decisions that create a premium feel, what belongs above the fold, and the ONE mistake that instantly makes a DIY site look cheap.”
Why it works: Making Claude ask questions first stops it from guessing your brand and defaulting to the safest, most generic answer. Studies show users form trust judgments about websites in as little as 50 milliseconds, before they’ve read a single word, and that snap judgment is almost entirely emotional and aesthetic. Context up front is what closes that gap.
2. The Anti Sameness Type Prompt
“Act as a typography director. My site currently uses [describe fonts]. Give me: a font pairing that feels intentional instead of default, a full type scale for headings, subheadings, body text and buttons, the right line height and letter spacing for each, and the ONE typography habit that makes even good content look amateur.”
Why it works: Default fonts are one of the biggest giveaways of a low effort site. Research on AI generated design notes that seeing a default typeface paired with no other typographic choices is a strong signal the design was never intentionally styled. Asking for an actual scale, not just “make it nicer,” gives you numbers you can use immediately.
3. The Breathing Room Auditor
“Act as a layout critic reviewing my page: [upload screenshots]. Go section by section. Tell me: where it feels cramped, where it feels empty in the wrong way, the exact spacing changes that would make it feel more expensive, and why generous white space is one of the fastest signals of a premium build.”
Why it works: This isn’t a vague feeling. White space creates visual breathing room that improves readability, guides attention, and communicates sophistication, and strategic use increases content comprehension by 20 percent. Uploading real screenshots means the feedback is about your actual page, not generic theory.
4. The Purposeful Motion Prompt
“Act as an interaction designer. My site is mostly static. Give me 3 small hover or scroll interactions that add polish without needing custom animation work. For each, tell me exactly where it belongs, what triggers it, and why it improves perceived quality. Then tell me the line between a tasteful detail and a distracting gimmick.”
Why it works: Overdone motion is a dead giveaway of an AI build. One design breakdown points out that everything fading in, everything glowing on hover, and everything having a subtle transform when it enters the viewport is a common AI pattern, because AI generated sites often mistake motion for polish. This prompt keeps you on the tasteful side of that line.
5. The Case Study Framer
“Act as a presentation specialist. I want my work section to look like a design studio’s case study page. Give me: the structure for presenting one project persuasively, exactly what to show versus leave out, how much text to use, and a caption style that lets the work speak for itself.”
Why it works: Amateur portfolios dump every asset on the page. Studios curate and tell a story instead. This prompt forces Claude to think like an editor cutting things out, which is the actual skill that separates a case study from a gallery.
6. The Trust Signal Sweep
“Act as a pre launch reviewer trained to spot amateur tells. Here is my site description: [describe]. Give me: the 5 small details that separate polished from unfinished, the order to fix them in, and the ONE thing worth obsessing over even if everything else is done. Also flag anything that looks like a fake or generic trust signal instead of a real one.”
Why it works: Fake trust elements are one of the most damaging AI tells. One analysis found that AI built sites often include the structural elements of trustworthy sites like testimonials and certificates without real substance behind them. This prompt catches that before you publish, not after.
7. The Reference Anchor Prompt (bonus, most people skip this one)
“Anchor my site’s visual direction to this reference: [paste a screenshot or link of a site you admire]. Match its type scale, spacing rhythm, and how it uses a single accent color, but do not copy it. Write real, specific copy for MY business, not filler lines. Tell me what you changed and why.”
Why it works: Vague requests get you the statistical average of every design Claude has seen. Design critics have flagged that AI generated layouts look familiar because they are statistically normal, and visual hierarchies technically function but feel like they were made for no one in particular. Feeding in a real reference is the single fastest way to break out of that average.

📌 Pro Tips
- Never let Claude design, write copy, and code all in one prompt. Split it into taste first, structure second, code last.

- Always paste a real reference image or link before asking for a redesign. Describing a vibe from memory keeps you stuck at “average.”

- Ask for exact numbers (font sizes, spacing values, hex codes) instead of vague words like “modern” or “clean.”

- Replace every placeholder image with a real photo or screenshot. Generic stock visuals are one of the fastest ways a site loses trust.

- Run the Trust Signal Sweep before every single publish, not just once. Treat it as a habit, not a one time step.

- Keep motion to one deliberate detail per section. If everything moves, nothing feels special.

🕵️ The Truth
A site doesn’t look premium because it cost a lot. It looks premium because someone made deliberate choices and left things out on purpose. The result of skipping this is design averaging, where layouts feel familiar because they’re statistically normal, and visitors can’t always articulate it, but they feel the absence of a real point of view. Claude can absolutely get you to studio quality, but only if you feed it real references, tight constraints, and a critic’s eye instead of a vague “make it look nice” request. The tool isn’t the bottleneck. The decisions you feed into it are.

Your mind decides if you stay stuck or get free. Most people never learn this in school.
“Never forget that fear and desire can lead you into life’s biggest trap if you are not aware of them controlling your thinking.”

Fear makes you say no to good opportunities. Desire makes you say yes to bad ones.
Tip: before any big decision, write down if you are acting from fear, from excitement, or from a clear plan. If it is fear or desire talking, wait 24 hours before deciding.
“Your mind is your greatest asset. It can also be your greatest liability.”

The same brain that builds a business can also talk you out of trying.
Tip: feed your mind daily with one piece of financial education, a podcast, a book chapter, or a video, instead of pure entertainment. What you feed grows.
“The poor and the middle class work for money. The rich have money work for them.”

Trading hours for a paycheck has a ceiling. Money working for you does not.
Tip: pick one small amount you can set aside monthly and put it into something that can grow or pay you back, even if it feels tiny at first.
“Assets put money in your pocket. Liabilities take money out of your pocket.”

This one sentence can fix most people’s finances.
Tip: list everything you own on paper. Next to each item write “pays me” or “costs me.” Be honest, this list will surprise you.
“The rich buy assets. The poor only have expenses. The middle class buy liabilities they think are assets.”

A car, a bigger house, or a new phone can feel like progress but often just drains cash.
Tip: before buying something big, ask “does this pay me back over time, or just cost me over time.” Then decide.
“A job is a short-term solution to a long-term problem.”

A job pays today’s bills but rarely builds lasting freedom on its own.
Tip: use your current job as fuel, not the finish line. Learn a skill or start a side project while you still have that steady paycheck.
“The rich don’t work for money. They make money work for them.”

This is the same idea repeated because it is the hardest habit to build.
Tip: every time you get paid, decide first how much goes toward something that can earn more money, then spend what is left.
“Schools teach you how to be a good employee.”

School teaches valuable skills but rarely teaches money management or investing.
Tip: treat financial education as a separate subject you study on your own, one article, one video, one lesson at a time.
“Wealth is how long you can survive without working.”

Real wealth is not your income, it is how many months you could go without a paycheck and still be fine.
Tip: calculate your number right now. Divide your savings by your monthly expenses. That is your current wealth score.
“Don’t work for money. Work to learn.”

Early in your career, chase skills and experience over salary. Skills compound, a single paycheck does not.
Tip: before taking or leaving a job, ask “what will I learn here” not just “what will I earn here.”

- Read one finance book every two months, start with this one if you have not already

🧠 PART 1: WHAT IS MONEY, REALLY?
- The Wealth of Nations | Adam Smith The OG economics book. Division of labor, trade, the “invisible hand.” Every other book on this list builds on ideas from here.

- The Economics of Money, Banking and Financial Markets | Frederic Mishkin Textbook but worth it. Learn how banks actually create money through lending. Once you get this, news about interest rates stops sounding like noise.

- Rich Dad Poor Dad | Robert Kiyosaki The classic starter book. Assets vs liabilities. “Make money work for you.” Simple but it plants a seed you’ll need later.

- The Richest Man in Babylon | George Clason Ancient parables, timeless lessons. “Pay yourself first” started here. Every money guru today is just repeating this book.

- Your Money or Your Life | Vicki Robin Reframes money as “life energy.” Once you calculate the real hourly cost of your job, you’ll never spend the same way again.

📈 PART 2: HOW MONEY MOVES (CYCLES, CRASHES, BIG PICTURE)
- This Time Is Different | Reinhart & Rogoff 800 years of financial crises. Same pattern every time. Debt builds up, everyone says “this time is different,” then it crashes anyway.

- The Changing World Order | Ray Dalio Bridgewater founder breaks down how empires rise and fall through debt cycles. Heavy but eye opening.

- Mastering the Market Cycle | Howard Marks Oaktree founder’s practical guide. “The most dangerous words in investing: this time it’s different.” Simple, sharp, no fluff.

- The Big Short | Michael Lewis Reads like a thriller but it’s the 2008 crash explained through real people who saw it coming. Painfully entertaining.

- Manias, Panics, and Crashes | Charles Kindleberger Every bubble follows the same script. Greed, mania, panic, crash. Understanding this pattern protects your money for life.

🧩 PART 3: WHY YOU KEEP LOSING MONEY (IT’S NOT MATH, IT’S YOU)
- The Psychology of Money | Morgan Housel 20 short stories proving wealth isn’t about being smart. It’s about behavior. This book alone will save you from dumb decisions.

- Thinking, Fast and Slow | Daniel Kahneman Nobel winner explains why your brain tricks you into bad money decisions. Loss aversion, bias, all of it explained here.

- Irrational Exuberance | Robert Shiller Why smart people make dumb bubbles together. Explains dot com crash, housing crash, and probably the next one too.

- Poor Economics | Banerjee & Duflo Nobel winning research that destroys the myth that poor people are just lazy. Understanding scarcity mindset changes how you see everyone, including yourself.

💰 PART 4: MAKING MONEY GROW (REAL STRATEGIES)
- Poor Charlie’s Almanack | Charlie Munger Multiple mental models, inverted thinking. “Tell me where I’m going to die so I don’t go there.” Practical wisdom disguised as a memoir.

- The Intelligent Investor | Benjamin Graham Buffett’s favorite book. Safety margins, market psychology, the roots of value investing. Dense but worth the slow read.

- A Random Walk Down Wall Street | Burton Malkiel The case for index funds explained simply. Most people trying to beat the market lose to people who just buy and hold.

- The Almanack of Naval Ravikant | Eric Jorgenson Wealth is not the same as money. Specific knowledge, leverage, and playing long games with long term people. Modern wealth building explained.

- Principles | Ray Dalio How to make decisions without ego getting in the way. Radical honesty applied to money, work, and life.

- The Black Swan | Nassim Taleb Rare events destroy fortunes. Learn to build a financial life that survives shocks instead of one that breaks from them.

✅ PRO TIPS
- Don’t read all 20 back to back. Pick 1 from each section first. That gives you a full mental map fast.

- Take notes by hand. Books about money hit different when you slow down enough to write the ideas out.

- Reread book 11 (Psychology of Money) every year. Your behavior changes as your income grows, and this book grows with you.

- Apply one idea per week. Reading without action is just entertainment.

- Skip books that don’t match your current stage. Beginners don’t need Dalio’s debt cycle theory yet. Start simple.

🔍 THE TRUTH
Nobody becomes wealthy by reading books. These books don’t hand you money. What they do is remove the fog. They teach you how money is created, how it moves, why markets crash, and why your own brain sabotages you.
Most people lose money not because they lack information but because they lack patience, discipline, and a clear framework. These 20 books together build that framework piece by piece. Economics gives you the foundation. Cycles give you timing awareness. Psychology explains your mistakes. Strategy gives you the tools.
Skip any one section and you’ll have blind spots. Skip psychology and you’ll keep repeating the same mistakes even with perfect knowledge. Skip cycles and you’ll get blindsided by crashes you could have seen coming.

- This month: add Psychology of Money and Rich Dad Poor Dad

- This quarter: tackle one cycle book (Housel recommends Marks for beginners)

- This year: work through the investing section slowly, one book every two months

- Ongoing: reread your favorites yearly, your understanding deepens each time

Money is not magic and it’s not random. It follows patterns, cycles, and psychology that have repeated for centuries. These 20 books are not a quick fix. They are a long term education that pays off for the rest of your life.
Start small. Pick one book. Read it this week. That’s it.

## Core Syntax: The @ Reference System
Seedance 2.0 uses `@` to assign roles to each uploaded asset. This is the most critical part of prompt writing.
### How to Reference
```
@Image1    @Image2    @Image3   ...
@Video1    @Video2    @Video3
@Audio1    @Audio2    @Audio3
```

Always explicitly state **what each reference is for**:
| Purpose | Example Syntax |
|---|---|
| First frame | `@Image1 as the first frame` |
| Last frame | `@Image2 as the last frame` |
| Character appearance | `@Image1’s character as the subject` |
| Scene/background | `scene references @Image3` |
| Camera movement | `reference @Video1’s camera movement` |
| Action/motion | `reference @Video1’s action choreography` |

| Rhythm/tempo | `video rhythm references @Video1` |

@Image1’s character as the subject, reference @Video1’s camera movement

|---|---|

|---|---|

|---|---|

## Common Mistakes to Avoid
1. **Vague references**: Don’t just say “reference @Video1” — specify WHAT to reference (camera? action? effects? rhythm?)
2. **Conflicting instructions**: Don’t ask for “static camera” and “orbit shot” in the same segment
3. **Overloading**: Don’t try to pack too many scenes into 4–5 seconds — keep it physically plausible
4. **Missing @ assignments**: If you upload 5 images, make sure each one is referenced with a clear purpose
5. **Ignoring audio**: Sound design dramatically improves output — always include audio direction
6. **Forgetting duration**: Match your prompt complexity to the selected generation length
7. **Real faces**: Don’t describe uploading real human photos — the system will block them

# Why This Content Goes Viral: Analysis
Before the system prompt, here’s the breakdown of the formula so you understand why it works (this logic is baked into the prompt below):
- The “Tiny Adult” Effect — The core comedic engine is babies/toddlers displaying adult emotional behaviors (pointing accusingly, crossing arms, scolding, pouting, standing with hands on hips) in response to trivial problems. This subverts our expectation that babies are helpless, which is inherently funny and infinitely relatable to parents.

- The Universal Micro-Story Arc — Every single video follows the exact same 6-beat emotional rollercoaster, compressed into 10-20 seconds:
Calm Normalcy → Disruption/Theft/Change → Dramatic Indignation → Adult Amusement (witness) → Resolution → Emotional Flip to Joy
This arc is satisfying because it mimics sitcom structure at hyper-speed — viewers get a complete emotional payoff in one scroll.

- The Witness Character — There’s almost always a third party (parent/adult) who laughs or reacts. This acts as a built-in “laugh track” / social proof cue that tells the viewer “this is funny,” priming their own reaction and making the clip more shareable.

- High-Empathy Conflict Sources — The “antagonist” is always low-stakes and adorable: a pet stealing an object, a parent cutting fruit, milk spilling, a page turning, sunlight disappearing. Nothing threatening — pure low-stakes comedy, safe to laugh at.

- Authenticity Signals (critical for AI-generated virality) — Static, low, handheld-feeling camera; no camera movement; natural/ambient lighting; “unedited home video” aesthetic; true-to-life color. These cues make the footage feel candid and real rather than produced, which is what makes it feel “found” rather than “made” — essential for AI video to pass as organic content.

- Sound-Design-First Writing — Every beat pairs a visual action with a specific sound cue (crying, laughing, fabric rustle, pouring, chewing). This ensures the video reads clearly even muted, but rewards sound-on viewing — maximizing watch-through on autoplay feeds.

- Pattern Recognition & Bingeability — Once a viewer sees one, they recognize the formula and seek more (pets + babies, “baby scolds X”), which drives profile follows and binge-watching — a series works better than a one-off.
